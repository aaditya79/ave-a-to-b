How we calculate risk

One worked example, on one street segment from the data: Central Park South in Manhattan, a 0.16-mile stretch of a collector street with no bike lane. Every number below comes from the same data the agents use.

1Count the crashes

Police reported 10 crashes that hurt a cyclist on this segment since January 2021, over about 5.8 years. 1 left a cyclist seriously hurt or killed.

A count this small moves a lot by chance, so we give it a 95% range: n ± 2 × √n from 10 crashes up, and the exact range for a count of rare events below 10 (0 crashes gives 0 to 3.7).

10 ± 2 × √10 = 3.7 to 16.3 crashes

Its own rate is 10 crashes over 0.91 mile-years (0.16 miles × 5.8 years), about 11.0 crashes per mile per year.

2Compare with similar streets

"Similar streets" are the same kind of street in the same borough: a collector street with no bike lane in Manhattan. There are 1,638 such segments, 93.6 miles in all.

Together they average 3.2 crashes per mile per year. At that rate this segment would have about 2.9 crashes since 2021, against the 10 it had.

3.2 × 0.91 mile-years = 2.9 expected

Even the low end of its range, 3.7, is above 2.9, so this segment stands out from similar streets beyond chance. That is the test every "stretch to watch out for" passes.

3Blend the two

A short segment has little data of its own, so its rate is pulled toward the similar-street average. How far depends on how much data it has and how much true rates vary between similar streets. Here the segment's own data gets 80% of the weight.

80% × 11.0 + 20% × 3.2 ≈ 9.5 crashes per mile per year

This blended estimate, 9.5, is what the routes use. A quiet one-block street with one crash stays close to its similar streets, and a long one with plenty of crashes keeps close to its own count.

4Estimate how many people ride it

Crashes alone favor empty streets, so we divide by an estimate of riders. Citi Bike trips nearby, scaled by the city's bike counters, give about 1,090 riders a day for this segment.

Nobody counts riders on most streets without bike lanes, so the share of riders a street like this carries is an assumption. We redo the math at half and at double that assumed gap between streets with and without lanes.

5Risk per mile ridden, with its range

Divide the blended crashes by the miles people ride here in a year:

9.5 ÷ (1,090 riders × 365 days) × 1,000,000 ≈ 23.7 crashes per million miles ridden

At the other two rider settings it is 11.9 and 94.9 per million miles ridden. Routes add this up along every segment, and a route is only called the safest of these three routes when it has the lowest risk at all three settings and its range doesn't overlap the others.

These numbers compare streets and routes. They are not anyone's personal odds, and they count only crashes the police recorded.
